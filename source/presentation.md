# SME Digital Transformation: Retail and Small Business

## Introduction

To understand what kinds of problems information systems can solve we must first understand what information systems are.  
Information systems is a combination of information technology and activities people do to support business processes, operations, management, and decision-making at different levels of the organization (Turban, Pollard and Wood, 2018). ISs goal is to improve workflows.
ISs collect inputs, manipulate data and generate outputs such as payrolls or customer orders and store the data for future use.

## Data and Information Management

ISs allow easy capture of key data required to support management of operations, think a point of sale system for example taht uses barcodes to manage products. This organisational data is processed into information used for: sales orders, reservations, stock control, payrolls, accounting, financial, marketing and inventory control to name a few (Turban, Pollard and Wood, 2018). This data can be used to form dashboards for better data visualisation and assist in financial decisions regarding the business. Changes that are not captured can result in data errors with financial impacts. Say for example a payment was sent to a supplier but no record was made by the business not only would the businesses financial statement be incorrect but the accounting department might pay the amount twice as it still reflects that it is unpaid. The use of ISs make errors like this harder to make as a record of the transaction would automatically be made.

Through Decision Support Systems (DSS) large amounts of data can be analysed and presented in an easy to understand way to enable users to make more informed decisions (Turban, Pollard and Wood, 2018). This includes strategic, operational and financial decisions.

Through the use of Supply Chain Management (SCM) systems organisations are able to coordinate supply and demand effectively and synchronise the movement of physical goods, payments, and data. SCM helps to optimise sourcing on materials and services at the lowest possible cost, manage inbound, outbound and internal product movement and use available resources effectively. SCM is designed with five key tasks (Ayers, 2006 as cited by Stanton D. 2023). That being designing supply chains for strategic advantage, implementing collaboration across departments, forging partnerships with supplier and customers, managing supply chain information, and aligning revenue and cost across the chain (Stanton D. 2023).

Information systems can use customer data to engage customers with personalised content to strengthen customer relations and tailor marketing campaigns and customer interaction to individual preferences. This all serves to increase customer satisfaction and keep customers coming back for more by fostering goodwill. This is done using Customer Relation Management tools that manage every interaction a business has with its customers to track buyer behaviour and customer-support interaction history.

## Data Ethics, Privacy, and POPIA compliance.

Privacy and legal obligations when collecting data.

Small retail shops gather personal data when clients make purchases, enroll in loyalty programs, order items online, or share their contact information. This data might consist of names, phone numbers, email addresses, shipping addresses, and transaction records. While gathering customer data can assist a business in enhancing its services, it also imposes an obligation to safeguard that data against misuse.

In South Africa, the Protection of Personal Information Act 4 of 2013 (POPIA) governs the processing of personal data. Companies need to have a legitimate justification for gathering and utilizing personal data, clarify its necessity, and manage it responsibly. Customers must not be deceived regarding the use of their information. A company should also refrain from gathering information that is not essential for its declared objective.

A small clothing store might request customers' names and email addresses when they enroll in a loyalty program. The store needs to clarify that this information will be utilized for handling rewards or sharing pertinent offers. It must not use the information for unrelated reasons without a valid legal reason. When consent serves as the foundation for processing, customers should have the ability to make an informed decision.

POPIA mandates that companies implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to safeguard personal data. This may involve utilizing robust passwords, limiting access to authorized personnel, ensuring software is current, and securely storing documents. Customer information in paper documents must be stored in a secure place instead of being accessible to anyone.

If an unauthorized person accesses or acquires personal information, the business might be required to inform the Information Regulator and the individuals affected as per POPIA. For instance, if an employee misplaces a device with customer information or an intruder accesses the store’s customer database, the company should examine the situation, implement measures to prevent additional damage, and adhere to the relevant reporting obligations.

Complying with POPIA goes beyond just steering clear of legal issues. It additionally aids a company in establishing customer confidence. Customers are more inclined to share information when they comprehend the purpose of its collection and trust that it will be managed responsibly.

Privacy and ethical obligations when collecting data

Data ethics pertains to the accountable and equitable gathering, retention, and utilization of data. A company can adhere to legal standards and still must evaluate if its practices are equitable to consumers. Responsible data management involves honoring privacy, being transparent about the usage of information, and steering clear of methods that could harm or mislead individuals.

Maintaining confidentiality is crucial for safeguarding customer data. Employees should access personal information solely when it is necessary for them to carry out their responsibilities. For instance, a cashier might need to handle a customer's transaction, but that doesn’t imply the cashier should check the customer's purchase history out of interest. The company needs to set explicit guidelines regarding who is permitted to access records and how they can use them.

Companies ought to utilize customer data ethically and responsibly. A retailer might examine sales data to determine trending items and enhance inventory levels. Nevertheless, caution is necessary when utilizing individual purchase histories to make customer-related decisions. Data must not be utilized in manners that unjustly discriminate against individuals, infringe on their privacy, or exceed what customers can reasonably anticipate.

Promoting products is yet another field where moral choices are significant. A store might aim to deliver offers to clients who enroll in its rewards program. It must clarify how their contact information will be utilized, honor their marketing choices, and offer a suitable method to cease receiving promotional communications. Customer information must not be shared or sold to other companies without a legitimate legal foundation.

Effective steps can assist a small retailer in safeguarding customer data. This involves educating workers on privacy, utilizing personal staff accounts rather than sharing passwords, securing devices when not in use, restricting access to customer information, and safely discarding documents that are no longer required. The company must possess a strategy for addressing potential data breaches.

In the end, responsible data handling safeguards both clients and the business's reputation. By handling personal data thoughtfully, a retailer can enhance its operations through digital systems while fostering accountable relationships with its clientele.

## Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Decision Support.

With the information systems (IS) in place the business gains the ability to leverage Decision support systems (DSS) to gather the information into useful marker and suggestions.
DSS can show graphs and projections that can aid the business in making decisions. Like seeing if a new strategy has had a effect on sales, or noticing when certain products go in or out of favor with your customers.
Artificial intelligence system go another step further by being taught pattern to make active suggestions to the managers. For example it may be a pattern that certain perishable products are sold less in winter months of the year. The AI system may notice this and suggest a month in advance to lower the order quantity for the month's order in anticipation of the lowered sold to expired ratios.
Both are critical to improve the efficiency of your business and would be a slow and inaccurate process without the support of the information systems (IS).

The best way to visualize the output of a DSS is with examples. It is a common pattern to see certain products going into fashion or falling out of fashion and it tends to take a while for a manager to notice and adjust prices to match.
A DSS collects the data per day or even per till visit and automatically creates helpful information viewing reports.
A graph showing that the prices are spiking above expected values is a clear way to notice these kinds of pricing changes early and empowering the store manager to up the prices or quickly order additional stock before stock runs out and the store misses out on revenue.
The same applies in reserve. A popular product going out of style can mean additional storage cost or even spoiling product that added to costs but will likely yielded no revenue leading to losses. A DSS trends graph can allow the store manager to notice this early and drop prices or plan for alternative uses, while lowering new orders to compensate, leading to a reduction in losses.

AI systems can use the DSS data to make decision in advance. For example a structured rule could be implemented to put in a order for new stock of a product if the stock of the store goes below a threshold value. Order bananas when there are fewer than 20 bananas left in the store and in storage. At the end of every day a store manager can see a list of low stock shown by an AI system so that they know what needs restocking.
They can also use collected data to compare supplier automatically. If supplier A sells bananas at a higher price than supplier B then the AI system can notice and prefer to select supplier B to restore the stock to healthy levels. At the end of every day the store manager can see not just what to restock but which the best supplier to buy from today would be.
There can be less structured decisions in place as well. Like setting a revenue and profits value compare to determine at what point the best way to expand the business is to open a new location. An AI system can track the values in the store and automatically suggest a new store location be opened once diminishing returns starts to appear significant in the current location. For example if customers per day has plateaued for too long. At the end of every day the Store owner can see when it becomes time to recommend opening a new store.

The data that DSS and AI systems can provide become more useful over time as well.
On a daily scale they can automate a lot of counting and management tasks like restocking, inventory tracking, and so on.
On a monthly scale they can identify which stocks to reinforce or diminish, which products would benefit most from a sales promotion, and more.
On a yearly scale they can show the need for new product categories or when the best time to open an additional store would be.
Though these systems do not replace the judgment of competent managers they do give your manager more time and information to make their decisions with and boost your business outcomes greatly.

Having data collected in one place means that a manager need only make the decisions and do the tasks that need to be done. There is not need for every manager to keep a abacus handy at the end of every day.

These Information Systems (IS) have limits and their value scales with the value of data fed into them.
The principle is "Garbage In Garbage Out" where it describes the idea that if the data being put into the system is inaccurate of limited in scope it directly affects the usefulness of the data generated by the systems.
If the stock counts or item prices are incorrectly entered then the system can not give an accurate recommendation or reflection of the state of your store. Similarly the system can not notice data that is not entered into it. For example items expiring. That could lead to a situation where the stock is miscounted due to unaccounted for additions or subtractions.
Similarly if only a small subset of products or sales are entered into the system it can only recommend the best decisions based on the incomplete data and would not be able to recommend a strategy around data it is not made aware of.
These systems can also not do all of the thinking for you. As useful as it is to have stock automatically counted and recommendation made, the data still requires a human to notice mistakes or plan for incoming changes to the market environment.

The simplest way to start taking advantage of them is to simply enter the values currently being kept track of on paper into a computer system where it can be saved and analyzed. Later on modern tills and automation products can be installed to input the data automatically. Card payments for example already require informing the bank of transactions. Adding in a step that informs a DSS then means no additional effort per transaction while also guaranteeing that the data be entered and tracked automatically.
The value of a DSS grows with the data you feed it so automated data collection slots nicely into that positive feedback loop.

DSS automation is by far the greatest value adding feature of implementing IS into your business as it

- Avoids losses
- Reduces costs
- Harnesses revenues
- Increases reactivity of your decisions
- Increases visibility of opportunities
- Keeps operational management simple
- Enables smart decision making
- And ultimately keeps manager time requirement consistent regardless of the scale of the business.

## Enterprise Systems and Information System (IS) Integration challenges.

What limitation can be expected and what challenges will present themselves.

Enterprise systems (such as ERP software) aim to bring the POS, inventory, finance and customer tools together into **one shared system**. Those parts only create value once they actually talk to each other, and that is exactly where integration gets difficult.

### Common limitations

- **Fragmented systems (data silos):** South African SME retailers typically run many separate apps (POS, stock, accounting, loyalty, payments) that do not integrate. In practice, they install dozens yet keep using only a handful. Reconciling them is manual and error-prone, which leads to spoiled stock, missed sales and insights that never get used.
- **No single source of truth:** when the till, the stock count and the books disagree, there is no reliable record to manage the business on.
- **Cascading errors:** because all the functions share one database, a single wrong entry or a bad migration spreads everywhere.

### Integration challenges

- **Process mismatch:** packaged systems are built around generic processes. When the software does not match how the business actually works, staff fall back to spreadsheets and workarounds.
- **Connection complexity:** linking the POS, e-commerce, suppliers and banks needs APIs and middleware. Every connection can break when either side changes, and each new tool adds logins, training and fees.
- **Cost blowouts:** data migration, integration, customisation and training cost far more than the licence fee. Professional setup alone can run to two or three times the annual licence, and real first-year projects overshoot their budgets (one SME budgeted R60,000 but ended up at R180,000).
- **Skills shortage and change resistance:** most South African businesses say skills shortages hold back digital transformation. Staff who are not trained simply bypass the system, so it never delivers its potential.
- **Infrastructure realities:** load-shedding, unreliable connectivity and rural coverage limit how much a business can rely on the cloud. Systems must keep working offline and sync when a connection returns.
- **Compliance and security:** POPIA and SARS (VAT) rules have to be built in from the start, and customer data must be protected.
- **High failure rate:** a large share of ERP implementations fail or disappoint. The cause is almost never the software; it is poor data, weak planning and under-investment in people.

## Adapting and Customizing Information Systems

Existing product offerings vs custom solutions and adapted help.

A retailer has three ways to get software, and the right choice depends on how standard the business processes are.

### Off-the-shelf products (buy)

- **Pros:** fast to deploy, predictable pricing, the vendor maintains and updates it, and it embeds industry best practice.
- **Cons:** limited fit (you bend your process to the software), feature bloat, and vendor lock-in with per-user pricing that grows with the business.
- **Best for:** standard functions every business does, such as accounting, payroll, POS and basic customer management.

### Custom solutions (build)

- **Pros:** built to your exact workflows, your data model and roadmap stay in your hands, and it can be a genuine competitive advantage.
- **Cons:** higher upfront cost, slower to deploy, and you own the maintenance, security and upgrades, so you need technical capacity or a reliable partner.
- **Best for:** the workflow that actually makes you money, when no product models it cleanly.

### Adapted help (configure and extend)

- **Rule of thumb: configure before you customise.** Most SME retailers should buy good commodity tools and configure them inside the vendor's limits, then build small pieces for the gaps using APIs or low-code connectors.
- Customisation is possible but expensive, and it can break every time the vendor releases an upgrade.

### A practical test

Count the spreadsheets sitting next to the current system. **Each spreadsheet marks a place where the software did not fit.** If the missing workflow is standard across your industry, buy the closest product. If it is what makes your business different, that is the part worth building or integrating.

## Practical Recommendation

A plan to implement IS into your business.

### Recommended plan for a small retailer

1. **Assess first:** map how money, stock and customers move through the shop, and define clear outcomes and success metrics before buying anything.
2. **Consolidate, don't accumulate:** choose one integrated system covering POS, inventory, accounting and customers (one or two tools that talk to each other) instead of adding more disconnected apps.
3. **Do vendor due diligence:** demand a live demo on your own data, check for local support, POPIA and SARS (VAT) readiness, offline capability for load-shedding, and references from similar retailers.
4. **Clean your data before migrating:** duplicate and incomplete records will wreck even a good system. Migrate only what the business needs and test the migration.
5. **Configure, then customise:** start from the vendor's defaults and only build what is genuinely unique to the business.
6. **Train by role and manage change:** train each person on the tasks they actually do, appoint a super user in the shop, and involve staff early so they take ownership.
7. **Go live in phases:** pilot one till or branch first, verify the results, then roll out to the rest. Keep the old records accessible during the move and arrange concentrated support (hypercare) for the first few weeks.
8. **Review and improve:** compare key indicators such as stock accuracy, cash-up differences, turnover and margins against the baseline, fix repeated problems, and plan the next phase.

### Realistic budgeting

The complete cost is more than the monthly fee. Plan for licensing, setup (often two to three times the annual licence), data migration, customisation, training and hardware, and add a **50–100% contingency** for the first year.

## Conclusion

Paper and spreadsheets keeps your data separated and limited to the efforts of singular people.
Information Systems (IS) keep data in a central location with analytics and archiving provided at minimal time investment from each worker at your stores.
They allow for the expansion of your boniness.

- More products.
- More people.
- More locations.
- No additional time per product.
- No additional time per person.
- No additional time per location.

Your boniness stays functional and accurately run without being restricted by the scattering of information and management critical data over every scaled up resource your business utilizes.

IS cost some money to implement and maintain, but ultimately keeps your business afloat and manageable. Luckily they can be implemented in stages where the value added by IS outgrows the associated costs for the rest of time.
There is no doubt that adding in information systems means a bright and profit growing future for your business.
